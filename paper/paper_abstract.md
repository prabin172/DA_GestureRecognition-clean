## Full abstract

Pretraining a motion representation on abundant vision-captured skeleton data and transferring it to data-scarce body-worn inertial sensors is an attractive strategy for wearable gesture recognition, but *how* the representation should be pretrained — with a supervised, reconstruction, contrastive, or hybrid objective — is unsettled, and the field has drifted toward reconstruction/self-supervised objectives on the assumption that they are the uniformly safe choice under domain shift. We show instead that the *identity* of the unsafe objective is not a fixed property of the objective — it depends on how wide the gap is. We present a controlled study isolating the pretraining objective across five variants (scratch, supervised, reconstruction, contrastive, hybrid) while transferring an NTU RGB+D Transformer encoder to a small Xsens gesture dataset (22 gestures, 5 subjects) over a shared, skeleton-derived quaternion representation — a moderate device gap — under subject-held-out few-shot calibration. Objective utility is gap-gated: a 45-point within-domain accuracy spread compresses to a few points here. At this moderate gap, and against the prevailing assumption, the significant negative-transfer case is *pure masked reconstruction*, which transfers below random initialization at every shot count (clip-level McNemar p<0.001, three seeds), while the hybrid objective is robustly best and the label-supervised objectives — softmax and contrastive alike — are competitive and best-calibrated. Extending to two independent public datasets and a substantially wider gap — a true cross-modal wearable-IMU target with no skeleton-derived representation to fall back on — reverses this: the label-supervised objectives, dominant at small gaps, become the *worst* priors, while the hybrid remains safe. The negative-transfer culprit thus flips identity with gap width; a reconstruction component is the one ingredient that stays safe across both regimes tested. Layer-wise CKA shows representational alignment is necessary but not sufficient in either regime. The prior's more gap-stable value is calibration and data-efficiency: it converges faster and helps most (+27 points) with 0–1 enrolled subjects, washing out by four — though this cold-start advantage also disappears on a target with a strong representation of its own. An abstract controller driven by the recognizer's own posteriors shows compressed recognition differences compound into much larger task-level ones, and surfaces a second, orthogonal failure mode: under randomized task design the reconstruction objective compounds worst, but under the one best-justified, reliability-ranked task design the best-calibrated objective's rare, confident misclassifications dominate once safety-critical errors are penalized severely — no single objective is unconditionally safe once the stress test is realistic.

## Tight version (~180 words, if length-limited)

Transferring skeleton-pretrained motion encoders to data-scarce wearable IMUs is attractive, but which pretraining objective survives the modality gap is unsettled — the field assumes reconstruction/self-supervision is uniformly safe. We isolate the objective across five variants (scratch, supervised, reconstruction, contrastive, hybrid), transferring an NTU RGB+D encoder first to a small Xsens dataset (a moderate, skeleton-derived device gap) and then to two public datasets including a true cross-modal wearable-IMU target (no skeleton-derived fallback), under subject-held-out few-shot calibration. The negative-transfer culprit's *identity* is gap-dependent, not fixed: at the moderate gap, *pure masked reconstruction* is unsafe (below random init at every shot count, McNemar p<0.001) while label-supervised objectives are competitive and best-calibrated; at the wider, true cross-modal gap this reverses — label-supervised objectives become worst, and only the hybrid (reconstruction+supervision) stays safe in both regimes. The prior's more stable value is calibration and data-efficiency (+27 points at 0–1 enrolled subjects, washing out by four — though not on a representationally strong target). A controller on the recognizer's own posteriors shows recognition differences compound sharply into task-level ones, and that no single objective is safe under every stress test: the reconstruction objective compounds worst on average, but the best-calibrated objective's rare confident misfires dominate under a realistic, fixed task design with severe safety-critical penalties.
